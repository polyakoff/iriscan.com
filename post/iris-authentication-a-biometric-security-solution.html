Iris Authentication: How Accurate It Is and How to Tune It

Why do the largest identity programmes, from India's Aadhaar to the UAE border control, rely on iris? The answer is statistical. Iris separates different people with a margin that other common modalities do not have, and this is what allows searching a database of millions without drowning in false matches. Below I explain where this margin comes from, what reduces it in real deployments and which settings are in your hands.

Why iris separates people so well

An iris code is a long string of bits (see how iris recognition works). Compare codes from two different eyes and about half the bits differ, simply because unrelated bits agree by chance half the time. The spread around that half is narrow: with hundreds of effectively independent bits, it is very unlikely for two different irises to agree on, say, 70% of them.

Codes from the same eye, taken on different days, differ much less, usually in 10–20% of bits. Blur, eyelids and a different pupil size push that number up, but rarely anywhere near the impostor range.

Large evaluations bear this out. Daugman's analysis of 200 billion comparisons from the UAE border system, and NIST's IREX programme, both show impostor distributions so tight that a threshold can be placed with a false match rate well below one in a million per comparison while still accepting the great majority of genuine attempts.

Verification and identification are different problems

Verification (1:1) answers "is this the person they claim to be?" One comparison, one decision. A false match rate of one in a million is far more than enough.

Identification (1:N) answers "who is this?" or "is this person already enrolled?" The probe is compared with everyone in the database, so the chance of at least one false match grows with N. A false match rate of one in a million per comparison means roughly one false hit per search in a database of a million people. For 1:N the threshold has to be stricter, and it should tighten as the database grows. Iris works well here because its impostor distribution is narrow enough to allow a strict threshold without rejecting too many genuine users.

What actually drives errors

  • Capture quality. Most false rejections come from blurred frames, half-closed eyes and off-axis gaze. A scanner that checks quality before accepting a frame, plus an operator who knows to ask for a second look, does more for accuracy than any matcher upgrade.
  • Usable iris area. When eyelids and lashes cover a large part of the iris, fewer bits are compared and the score becomes less reliable. Our Cloud Iris API returns the occluded share with every enrolment, so you can retake an image before it becomes a bad template.
  • Enrolling both eyes. Two irises give two independent chances. Search with either eye, and require both when the stakes are high.
  • Cross-device use. Templates captured on one scanner and compared with templates from another score slightly worse. Test with the actual hardware mix you will deploy.
  • Time. The iris is stable, but match scores drift a little over years, mostly due to changing pupil dynamics and different cameras. Long-running systems refresh templates when a person is verified anyway.

Spoofing

Iris is hard to spoof covertly: you cannot lift an iris from a glass the way you can a fingerprint. Known attacks use printed iris images, patterned contact lenses and replayed video. Countermeasures check for things a print cannot do: pupil reaction to light, specular reflections in the right places, texture of a real cornea. If your threat model includes deliberate attacks, require presentation attack detection tested against ISO/IEC 30107-3 and keep an operator in the loop where you can.

How to choose the threshold

Do not take a threshold from a datasheet. Run the system on a sample of your own population and hardware: enrol a few hundred people, collect genuine attempts on a different day, and compute the two distributions. Choose the false match rate your use case can tolerate, read the threshold off the impostor curve, and check what false rejection rate it gives you. For 1:N, repeat the check whenever the database grows by an order of magnitude.
