One Person, One Identity: Biometrics in Elections and at the Border

What do a voter register and a border have in common? Both are public registers that work only if each person appears in them once, and both are checked at a single moment of decision: a polling station on election day or a passport booth at arrival. Both fail in the same way, through duplicate records and impersonation. Below I look at three experiences, Nigeria's elections, the UAE border and the new EU Entry/Exit System, and what a government can take from them.

Nigeria: cleaning the register and checking the voter

Nigeria's voter register was once compiled manually and was widely believed to be bloated with under-age and duplicate registrations. The Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC) moved to biometric registration for the 2011 elections, and for 2015 it introduced chip-based permanent voter cards and smart card readers at polling units.

The first lesson came from the readers. According to International IDEA, a mock accreditation exercise showed success rates of up to 92% in some states, around 36% in others and only 3.4% in one. Authentication of fingerprints was more difficult for some voters, there were cards with faulty chip encoding and cards that were mishandled. A technology that fails for two thirds of voters in one state and works in another is not neutral: it changes who can vote.

The second lesson came from the register. Before the 2023 elections INEC ran new registrations through its Automated Biometric Identification System. In October 2022 its chairman announced that 2.78 million of the new registrations had been removed as invalid, including double and multiple registrations, under-age persons and fake records, and that 9.5 million new valid registrations brought the register to 93.5 million voters. This is exactly what biometric de-duplication is for: catching the same person under different names before the register is published.

The UAE: iris at the border since 2001

The United Arab Emirates started using iris recognition at its air, land and sea ports in 2001 to stop persons barred from the country from coming back, even with a new passport. According to John Daugman of the University of Cambridge, whose algorithms the system uses, about 12,000 arriving travelers are compared against a watch list of about a million iris templates every day, which is about 12 billion comparisons per day. He reports 73,180 matches so far against the watch list among people seeking re-entry.

This case is important for one reason: it shows that an exhaustive search against a large watch list is practical without drowning border officers in false alarms. A new passport does not help a person whose iris is already in the list.

The EU: from stamps to biometric records

On 12 October 2025 the European Union started the Entry/Exit System at the external borders of 29 countries. Non-EU travelers on short stays now register their passport data, a facial image and fingerprints at their first crossing, and later crossings need only a quick verification. Since 10 April 2026 the system works at all external border crossing points and has replaced passport stamps. Among its stated purposes are detecting overstayers and cases of document and identity fraud.

The EU had passport stamps and document checks for decades. It moved to biometrics because a stamp in a forged or second passport tells nothing about the person.

Lessons for governments

  • De-duplicate before you verify. A checkpoint can only confirm that a person matches a record. If the register already contains the same person twice, verification will confirm both.
  • Measure failures by region. The Nigerian numbers show that failure rates differ hugely by location, hardware and population. Pilot in the hardest region, not the easiest.
  • Plan a fallback that cannot be abused. A voter whose fingerprints fail must still be able to vote, and a traveler must still be processed. The fallback should be logged and reviewed, otherwise it becomes the new route for fraud.
  • Use more than one modality. Iris for the people whose fingerprints are worn, face for compatibility with travel documents, fingerprints for compatibility with existing registers.
  • Keep control of the data. A register is a national asset. Open standards and open platforms such as MOSIP mean that the next government can change the vendor without re-enrolling the country.
  • Make it auditable. In elections trust matters as much as accuracy. Observers should be able to see how many records were removed and why.

What biometrics does not solve

Biometrics cannot fix a vote count, prevent vote buying or decide who is entitled to cross a border. It answers one question, whether this person is the person in the record, and it answers it only as well as the register is built. Biometric registers of whole populations also carry obvious risks for privacy and civil liberties, so the legal basis, the limits of use and the retention rules have to be set by law before the first person is enrolled, not after.
