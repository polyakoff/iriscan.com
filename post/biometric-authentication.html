Biometric Authentication: Modalities, Templates and Lock-in

Biometric authentication checks who someone is by something they are, not something they know or carry. Every system does it the same way: capture a sample, reduce it to a template, compare templates. The modality you choose decides accuracy and cost. The template format you choose decides whether you can ever change suppliers.

The three modalities that matter

Face. Works with any camera, which makes it the default for phones and self-service. A neural network turns an aligned face into an embedding, a vector of a few hundred numbers; two faces match when their vectors are close. Good for 1:1 verification. Weaker for searching large databases, and needs liveness detection, because a photo of a face is easy to get.

Fingerprint. The most widely deployed modality, with forty years of standards behind it. Templates store minutiae: the points where ridges end or split, with their positions and angles. Accurate and cheap to capture, but quality drops for manual workers and the elderly, and contact sensors need cleaning.

Iris. The most distinctive of the three. A near-infrared camera captures the iris texture, which is encoded into a bit string and compared by Hamming distance. Best for de-duplicating large populations, contactless, stable over life. Needs a dedicated scanner. (Details: how iris recognition works.)

Voice, vein and behavioural biometrics exist and have their niches, but national ID, banking and humanitarian programmes are built almost entirely on these three, often combined.

Templates, not images

After enrolment, a biometric system mostly works with templates. A fingerprint template is a list of minutiae; an iris template is a bit string with a mask; a face template is an embedding. Templates are small, fast to compare, and cannot be turned back into the original image, which makes them the safer thing to store and exchange.

That raises the question that decides the long-term cost of the system: in what format are the templates stored?

Where lock-in comes from

Many vendors store templates in a proprietary format that only their own matcher reads. As long as you stay, it works. If you want a better matcher, a cheaper scanner or a second supplier, you find that your database is useless to anyone else. You either re-enrol everyone or keep paying.

The fix has existed for years: international standards for template interchange.

  • Fingerprint minutiae: ISO/IEC 19794-2 and ANSI INCITS 378;
  • Face images: ISO/IEC 19794-5;
  • Iris images: ISO/IEC 19794-6;
  • and the newer ISO/IEC 39794 series that replaces them with an extensible encoding.

Even within standards, details differ: ISO stores minutia angles in units of about 1.4°, ANSI in 2°, and coordinates depend on the declared resolution. Converting correctly between them is fiddly but routine, and it should not require a licence.

On the device or on the server

Matching on the user's phone keeps biometric data off your servers entirely, works offline and costs nothing per check. It is the right choice for 1:1 verification in an app. Searching a database (1:N) has to happen where the database is, on your servers or a trusted service. Most real systems combine both: enrol once with a search for duplicates, then verify on the device.

What we build

Iriscan's components are open source and use standard formats throughout. The Biometric SDK does face, liveness and iris on Android, iOS, JVM and React Native. The Biometric Converter turns fingerprint images into ISO or ANSI templates and converts templates between the two; it runs as a C library, a Docker service or in the browser. The Cloud Iris API handles iris enrolment and search.
