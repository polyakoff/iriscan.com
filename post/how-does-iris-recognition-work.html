How Iris Recognition Works

An iris recognition system does five things: it takes a near-infrared picture of the eye, finds the iris in it, unrolls the iris into a rectangle, turns its texture into a string of bits and compares that string with others. Each step is simple to describe. The quality of the whole system depends on how carefully each one is done.

1. Capture

Iris cameras photograph the eye in near-infrared light, roughly 700–900 nm. In visible light a dark brown iris looks almost uniform. In infrared the same iris shows its crypts, furrows and rings, because melanin absorbs much less infrared than visible light. Infrared also takes ambient lighting out of the equation: the camera brings its own illumination, so a picture taken in a sunny office and one taken in a dim tent look alike.

The standards ask for about 150–200 pixels across the iris. A dedicated scanner, such as an Iritech IriShield on a USB cable or a Vista EY2 on a desk, gets there at 20–40 cm. A phone camera, without infrared illumination, usually does not, which is why phones use face rather than iris.

A good capture device also checks quality before it keeps a frame: focus, motion blur, how open the eye is, gaze direction. Most errors in iris recognition start here, so a scanner that refuses a blurry frame saves trouble later.

2. Segmentation

The software then locates two boundaries: the pupil (inner) and the limbus, where the iris meets the white of the eye (outer). Classic algorithms fit circles with an integro-differential operator or a Hough transform. Newer ones use neural networks and fit ellipses, which handles off-axis gaze better.

It also marks what is not iris: eyelids, eyelashes, reflections of the illuminator. These areas go into a mask. They are not deleted, just excluded from comparison later.

3. Unwrapping

The pupil dilates and contracts, and the eye is never at exactly the same distance from the camera. To compare two irises, the ring between the two boundaries is mapped onto a fixed rectangle: angle along one axis, distance from pupil to limbus along the other. This is John Daugman's "rubber sheet" model. After it, a point on the iris lands in the same cell of the rectangle whatever the pupil size.

4. Encoding

The rectangle is filtered with Gabor or similar wavelets, and for each point the sign of the filter response is kept as a bit or two. Only the phase survives; contrast and brightness are thrown away, which makes the code robust to lighting and camera differences. The result is an iris code, a fixed-length string of bits (2048 in Daugman's original design) plus a mask of the same length.

An iris code is not an image. You cannot turn it back into a picture of the eye, and it contains no name, age or eye colour. That matters for privacy and for where templates are allowed to travel.

5. Comparison

Two iris codes are compared by Hamming distance: the share of bits that differ, counted only where both masks say the bits are valid. Codes from the same eye typically differ in 10–20% of bits. Codes from different eyes differ in close to half, because unrelated bits agree by chance half the time. To allow for head tilt, the comparison is repeated with one code shifted a few positions left and right, and the best result is taken.

A threshold decides between match and non-match. Because the two distributions are so far apart, iris can search very large databases with few false matches. How to choose the threshold is a separate topic, covered in Iris authentication: how accurate it is and how to tune it.

Where each step lives in practice

In a deployment these steps are spread across hardware and software, and the split decides a lot about cost and lock-in:

  • Capture happens on the scanner and in its driver. Our Edge service wraps Iritech scanners and gives a web page a local API for live preview, quality score and capture.
  • Segmentation, unwrapping and encoding can run on the edge device, on a phone or on a server. The open-source Iriscan Biometric SDK does it on Android, iOS and the JVM.
  • Comparison against a database runs where the database is. The Cloud Iris API enrols left and right eyes per person and offers 1:N search and 1:1 verification; the Edge service keeps an encrypted local copy so matching works offline.

Whatever runs where, keep the templates in a standard record format (ISO/IEC 19794-6 or its successor 39794-6). If templates are stored in a vendor's private format, changing the matcher later means re-enrolling everyone.
